# Two credential shapes that are published as documentation

Nine of the ten shipped rules carry an entropy floor, which is usually what a
documentation example fails: it drops `AKIAXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXX` at 1.0219 bits per
byte. Two rules are not covered by it, and for both the published example is
byte-identical to the real thing.

**`private-key-block` was the third and is done.** It matched the PEM header
alone, and the rule now requires a base64 body line after it — the first of the
three answers below, taken because it was measured rather than argued. Scanned
over every tracked file the rule went from 3 hits to 1, the remaining one being
its own planted fixture; the two it lost were this row and `rules/README.md`,
both describing the defect. What follows is the residue.

**`jwt`** matches three base64url segments with the first two opening `eyJ`, at
a floor of 3.5 bits. The jwt.io sample token measures 5.4441 and clears it, and
that token is pasted into a very large number of READMEs. A real JWT and a
sample JWT are the same object; only the signing key differs, and the scanner
cannot see it. No floor separates them — this one needs claim decoding.

**`aws-access-key-id`** is the same shape from the other direction, and it was
found by scanning this repository rather than by reading the rules. AWS's own
documented `AKIAIOSFODNN7EXAMPLE` measures 3.6842 against a floor of 3.0, so it
passes by design: the floor exists to drop `AKIAXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXX` at 1.0219,
and a realistic-looking published example is exactly what it cannot drop. It
fires on `testdata/corpus/README.md` here, and on any page quoting the AWS
docs.

A denylist is the obvious answer and carries `card-placeholder`'s hazard,
stated in that code: an entry that is not genuinely a published example
suppresses a real credential, and that failure is invisible. The card denylist
is bounded by two gateways' primary tables; there is no equivalent boundary for
"example keys" across every vendor.

Neither has a case in `testdata/corpus/`, so the `precision` gate is silent
about both. That is the first thing to fix: plant the two documents in the
clean half and read what happens, which will be two findings and a red gate.
What to do about it is the decision.

Three answers, and the corpus is what chooses between them.

- **Require more than the header.** A private key is a header plus a base64
  body plus a footer; requiring the body line makes a bare quoted header quiet
  and costs nothing real, since a key without its body is not a key. RE2 has no
  lookaround but does have `\n`, so this is expressible.
- **Fold in a placeholder denylist**, as `card-placeholder` already does for
  payment cards. For `jwt` that is a handful of published sample tokens; the
  argument against is `card-placeholder`'s own — see [Q33](Q33.md) — that a
  vendor's list has no boundary.
- **Drop the rule.** `jwt` is the weaker of the two: a JWT in a transcript is
  usually already expired, and the rule's recall is bought at a precision cost
  nothing else in the ruleset pays.

What is not an answer is an exception in the corpus. A rule that needs one is a
rule to drop, which is the whole argument for pinning the clean count at zero.
